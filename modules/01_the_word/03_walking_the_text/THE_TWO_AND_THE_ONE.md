# The Two and the One

**Status:** Draft | **Author:** Brandon O'Shields | **Last updated:** 2026-08-01

---

> So God created man in his own image, in the image of God he created him; male and female he created them. — Genesis 1:27

If [To See](/the-word/walking-the-text/to-see) is right — that the deepest failure between people is projecting an image instead of receiving the person — then the hardest and most important place to test it is the oldest relationship of all: man and woman. Almost everything that has gone wrong between the sexes, and almost everything the modern world argues about so bitterly, comes down to a failure to see. We relate to an image of the other — a fear, a grievance, a caricature handed to us by our history or our tribe — instead of to the person actually standing there. This is the beginning of the healing, and it has to be said before anything else: *you cannot rightly relate as man and woman until you can see one another as you are.*

## The dyadic image

Begin where Scripture begins. "In the image of God he created him; *male and female* he created them." The image of God is not carried by the solitary man alone, nor the solitary woman alone. It is carried by the two together — a *dyad*. Humanity images a God who is Himself communion, and it does so in the union and difference of male and female. The difference is not a ranking. It is the very shape of the image. Two who are genuinely other, and genuinely one.

Then Genesis 2 gives us the word that undoes centuries of misreading. God says it is not good for the man to be alone, and He makes an *ezer kenegdo* (Genesis 2:18) — usually flattened in English to "a helper suitable for him." But *ezer*, "helper," is a word Scripture most often uses of **God Himself** as Israel's helper and deliverer — never a word for a subordinate or an assistant. And *kenegdo* means "corresponding to, facing, opposite him" — a partner who stands face to face with him, a strength that meets his strength. Read in its own language, *ezer kenegdo* is nearer to "a corresponding strength, facing him" than to "a little help around the house." The woman is not beneath the man and not a copy of him. She is his equal-and-facing other — and note the word: *facing.* The first marriage is defined as a **face-to-face** relationship. The dyad is built for seeing.

And then: "the two shall become one flesh." Not one absorbing the other, and not two who stay strangers sharing an address, but two distinct persons made one — difference for the sake of communion, not for the sake of hierarchy.

## Naming the polarization

Now hold that biblical picture up against the way our culture fights about men and women, because the fight is real and most people feel trapped between two camps. It helps to name them plainly — and to notice that each one begins from a real wound.

**One pole reads difference as ranking.** It says the man is the head in the sense of command, the woman under his authority, and the right ordering of the home is a chain of control. This camp is usually reacting to something true: the collapse of male responsibility, fathers who abdicated, households that dissolved when no one would take up the weight of leadership. Its instinct to recover male responsibility is not wrong. But its distortion is severe: it collapses authority into domination, and it mutes the God-given strength and agency of the woman — the very *ezer* strength Scripture honors. It reads Genesis as a hierarchy when Genesis gives a dyad.

**The other pole reads equality as sameness.** It says difference itself is the problem — that "roles" are oppressive fictions, that man and woman are interchangeable, and that liberation means erasing the distinction. This camp, too, is reacting to something true: generations of real domination, women diminished, gifts buried, abuse excused by appeals to "headship." Its protest against that is righteous. But its distortion is also severe: it erases the given, embodied difference that the image of God is carried in, and it makes the self the sole author of its own meaning — the very [expressive individualism](/the-word/knowing-your-own-way/the-cultural-currents) that dissolves covenant everywhere it touches.

Here is the thing almost no one says: **both poles grew in the same soil.** Both are downstream of the modern frame that turned every relationship into either a power-hierarchy or a market of autonomous individuals. One camp keeps the hierarchy; the other keeps the autonomy. Neither has recovered the *dyad* — two distinct, equal-in-dignity, interdependent image-bearers whose difference is for communion. The whole argument is being conducted inside a broken frame. Which means you do not have to win it. You can step behind it.

## What we are not saying

So let this be plain, because it is the point where people reach to sort you into a camp. This is **not** a claim that men rank above women. And it is **not** a claim that men and women are interchangeable. Scripture gives neither the ladder nor the blur. It gives the dyad: real difference, real equality, real union, ordered toward communion and fruitfulness.

Does that leave room for leadership and authority? Yes — but not the kind either camp imagines. There is real leadership in a covenant home, and real honor owed, and real responsibility carried. But covenantal authority is **sacrificial, relational, and accountable** — the kind that lays itself down for the other — never ranked control. Hierarchy in the sense of domination is foreign to Scripture; leadership in the sense of glad, sacrificial responsibility runs right through it. This is the same covenantal grammar of authority that orders the whole of public life in [The Compact](/the-compact) — authority that is personal and accountable before it is procedural, that serves rather than rules.

## Domination is the enemy, in either direction

And this must be said without hedging, because so many have been wounded here. **Domination is the sin the whole vision exists to heal — and it runs both ways.** A husband who imposes his will on his wife and a wife who imposes hers on her husband are doing the identical thing: treating a covenant partner as territory to control rather than a face to behold. Authority that dominates has already stopped being covenantal; it has become the very thing Christ came to overturn. If you have been on the receiving end of control dressed up as "biblical order," hear this clearly: that was not the order Scripture teaches. It was its counterfeit. The recovered vision is not a subtler tool for control. It is the end of control — because, as [To See](/the-word/walking-the-text/to-see) showed, *you cannot dominate what you truly see.* The moment you behold your spouse as an image-bearer of God, with a face and a weight equal to your own, using them becomes unthinkable.

## The ache, and the beginning of the cure

Most couples do not feel this argument as theology. They feel it as an ache: *you don't really see me.* He feels unseen in his labor and his longing to be trusted; she feels unseen in her strength and her weariness and her worth. Each has, over years, built an image of the other and quietly begun relating to the image — and the image is always a little unfair, a little frozen, a little less than the living person. The cure is not a better division of chores or a clearer chain of command. It is the slow, humbling recovery of sight: setting down the image, and going looking for the face. Asking of your husband, your wife, the same honest questions you learned to ask of a text — *who are they really, in their own world, apart from what I have projected?* — and then doing the unglamorous work of receiving the answer, for a lifetime.

## Outward: the first school of seeing

This is why the home matters so far beyond itself. The marriage is the **first school of seeing** — the place where two people learn, across a lifetime, to behold an other face to face and not flinch. Children raised in that home learn what it is to be truly seen, and so learn to see. And the seeing does not stop at the door. It scales outward: from spouse, to household, to the neighbor.

For the covenant life is finally about learning to *see our neighbors* — the stranger at the gate, the poor at the edge of the field, the household down the road — as the real people they are, rather than as our projections, our threats, or our market segments. That widening of sight, from the household outward into public life, is the work of [The Compact](/the-compact): a whole community learning to behold one another face to face. The home is where that sight is first trained. A people who cannot see across their own kitchen table will not see across the square.

So the recovery of the two and the one is not a private matter. It is where a whole culture of seeing begins.
