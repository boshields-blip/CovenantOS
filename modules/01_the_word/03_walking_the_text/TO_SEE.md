# To See

**Status:** Draft | **Author:** Brandon O'Shields | **Last updated:** 2026-08-01

---

> Now we see in a mirror dimly, but then face to face. Now I know in part; then I shall know fully, even as I have been fully known. — 1 Corinthians 13:12

There is one discipline underneath everything The Word has been teaching you, and it is time to name it. You have been learning to *see* — to read a passage in its own world rather than the frame you brought to it, to trace your own way honestly rather than the flattering picture of yourself, to weigh a tradition as it actually is rather than the caricature your tribe handed you. All of it is the same act: letting the other be other, and receiving it as it gives itself, rather than fixing it into an image of your own making.

That single discipline turns out to govern three things at once — how you read Scripture, how you know God, and how you see the person across the table. And the failure underneath all three is the same failure. Learn it in one, and you begin to learn it in all.

## The one failure: making an image

When you cannot see the text as it is, you project. You lift a verse out of its world and drop it into your assumptions, and you hear your own voice come back to you as though it were God's. When you cannot see a person as they are, you do the very same thing: you build an image of them — your husband, your wife, your father, your neighbor — out of your fears, your history, and your needs, and then you relate to the image instead of the person. You argue with the image. You resent the image. You never quite meet the one who is actually there.

Scripture has a word for making an image of what should be received living and free. It calls it idolatry. The second commandment forbids graven images of God for a reason that runs deeper than we usually notice: an image is something *we* fashion and *we* control. It holds still. It never surprises us, never corrects us, never asks anything of us we did not already grant it. The living God refuses to be reduced to that, because He is free, and He insists on being *received as He reveals Himself* rather than constructed as we prefer.

The same is true of a person. To make a fixed image of your spouse is a quiet idolatry — you have made something you can manage in place of someone you must keep meeting. It feels safer. It is also the death of love, because love requires that the other stay free, stay alive, stay capable of surprising you. You cannot love an image. You can only love the one who is really there, and only if you are willing to keep seeing them.

This is why misreading Scripture and misreading each other are not two problems but one. Both are the refusal to receive a living other. Both replace a face with an image.

## The word is *panim* — face

The Hebrew Scriptures do not speak abstractly about "seeing." They speak of the *face* — *panim*. To be in covenant is to be *face to face*. "The LORD make his face shine upon you and be gracious to you" (Numbers 6:25). Moses is the one whom the LORD knew "face to face" (Deuteronomy 34:10). Jacob wrestles through the night and names the place Peniel — "the face of God" — because, he says, "I have seen God face to face, and yet my life has been delivered" (Genesis 32:30). To seek God is to "seek his face" (Psalm 27:8).

Sin, in this grammar, is the averted face — turning away, hiding, refusing to be seen. After the first disobedience, the man and woman hide from the face of God, and they cover themselves from each other. The two hidings happen together, and they always do: when we cannot bear to be seen by God, we cannot rightly see each other either.

And salvation is the recovery of the face. "We all, with unveiled face, beholding the glory of the Lord, are being transformed into the same image" (2 Corinthians 3:18). We are changed by being seen and by learning to see. The whole long story bends toward the promise that we will one day know fully, "even as we have been fully known" — that we will at last be seen all the way through and, astonishingly, still be loved.

## Seeing and knowing are the same

Remember *yada*, from [the language of the land](/the-word/the-language-of-the-land/hebrew-word-pictures): to know, in Hebrew, is not to hold information about someone. It is to know them by lived, intimate, covenantal encounter — the difference between knowing *about* a person and knowing *them*. So to truly see someone is to *know* them, and to know them is to have received them as they are, over time, in their own context, rather than filed them under a label.

This is exactly what [Walking the Text](/the-word/walking-the-text/reading-in-context) has been training in you toward Scripture: the patience to ask who the first hearers were, what world they assumed, what the passage meant *to them* before you decide what it means to you. That patience — the willingness to be a receiver rather than a projector — is not a technique reserved for books. It is the posture of love. The person who has learned to read a text in its own context has been quietly trained in the very thing that lets them see a spouse in their own context: as a real other, with their own history, their own wounds, their own gifts, not reducible to my image of them.

To read Scripture rightly and to love a person rightly are, at the root, the same act of humble, contextual, self-emptying attention. This is why they rise and fall together. A person who is learning to receive the living God as He reveals Himself is being taught, in the same motion, to receive the living people He has placed across the table.

## Why this heals rather than wounds

Two things follow, and they matter especially where men and women, and whole households, have been wounded.

First, **you cannot dominate what you truly see.** Domination begins with an image — reducing the other to a role, a threat, an object, a thing you can use or control. The moment you genuinely see a person as an image-bearer of God, with a face and a name and an inner life as real and weighty as your own, using them becomes unthinkable. So the recovery of sight is not a subtle tool for control; it is the end of control. It is the thing that makes covenant love possible and makes domination impossible. Every place a household has been broken by one person imposing an image on another, the healing begins here — with the slow, humbling work of learning to see the actual person again.

Second, **being seen is what we most long for and most fear.** To be truly seen — all the way through, without the image we perform — is terrifying, because we are sure that if we were fully known we could not be loved. The gospel says the opposite. We are already fully known — "even as I have been fully known" — and, in Christ, fully loved anyway. That is the ground under everything. Because God has seen us all the way to the bottom and still set His face toward us in grace, we are freed from the exhausting work of managing our image, and freed to offer the same grace across the table: to see, and to let ourselves be seen, without hiding.

## Walking in this

Everything downstream in CovenantOS depends on this recovered sight, and it widens as it goes. The [household](/the-household) is, before anything else, the school where two people learn to see each other face to face across a lifetime — and where children learn what it is to be truly seen. From there the same sight reaches outward to the neighbor: the whole public life of [The Compact](/the-compact) is a community learning to behold one another — the stranger at the gate, the poor at the field's edge, the household down the road — as real people rather than as projections, threats, or market segments. The whole covenant life is one long apprenticeship in *panim*: refusing the image and receiving the person, first across the table, then across the square.

So this is the discipline to carry out of The Word and into your home: when you catch yourself relating to an image — of God, of Scripture, of your spouse, of your neighbor — set it down, and go looking for the face. Ask of a person the same honest questions you learned to ask of a text: who are they really, in their own world, apart from what I have projected? And then do the slow, patient, unglamorous work of receiving the answer.

To see is where love begins.
