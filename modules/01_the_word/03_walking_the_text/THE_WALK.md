# The Walk

**Status:** Draft | **Author:** Brandon O'Shields | **Last updated:** 2026-08-01

---

Here is the practice itself — a simple, repeatable way to walk any passage as its first hearers would. Then a worked example, so you can see it done before you try it on your own.

## The practice

Take a single passage — a story, a psalm, a paragraph of a letter. Read it once, slowly, out loud if you can. Then walk it:

1. **Name the genre.** What kind of writing is this, and how does that kind ask to be read?
2. **Picture the audience.** Who first received this? What was their situation?
3. **Ask the occasion.** Why was this written or spoken? What need was it meeting?
4. **Rebuild the assumed world.** What did the first hearers simply *know* — about covenant, household, land, honor, holiness — that I have to reconstruct? Where would they have gasped, or wept, or been scandalized, where I feel nothing?
5. **Locate it in the story.** Where does this stand in the covenant story, from creation to new creation?

Only *then*, having heard what they heard:

6. **Cross the bridge.** What does this passage, understood in its own world, ask of my household and my life now?

The order matters. The bridge to your life is the last step, not the first. Walk the text before you apply it.

## A worked example: the father and two sons (Luke 15:11–32)

Most of us have heard the parable of the prodigal son so many times it has gone flat. Walking it restores the shock its first hearers felt.

**1. Genre.** This is a parable — a story Jesus tells to reveal something, not a historical report. Parables work by setting up a familiar scene and then breaking it in a way that exposes the heart. So we should watch for the moment the story *breaks the expected pattern*. That break is usually the point.

**2. Audience.** Luke tells us plainly (15:1–2): Jesus is speaking to two groups at once — tax collectors and sinners drawing near, and Pharisees and scribes grumbling that He welcomes them. The parable is aimed at both the lost son *and* the elder brother in His hearing. Keep both in view.

**3. Occasion.** The occasion is the grumbling: "This man receives sinners and eats with them." The parable is Jesus' answer to a complaint about His table. That alone should catch the attention of anyone shaped by the Household-Covenant vision — the whole story is provoked by a question about who is welcome at the meal.

**4. The assumed world.** This is where the parable comes alive, and where a modern reader misses almost everything.

- When the younger son asks for his share of the inheritance *while his father is alive*, he is doing something very close to wishing his father dead. In that honor-shame world, it is a stunning insult — a public disgrace to the whole household. The first hearers would have expected the father to strike him, or disown him. Instead the father *gives* it. The scandal starts in verse 12.
- The son "squanders" the estate among Gentiles and ends up feeding pigs — for a Jewish audience, an image of total degradation, an Israelite reduced to serving the unclean. He has not just failed; he has unmade his belonging.
- When he returns, the father *runs* to him. We read this as touching. They would have read it as shocking. A dignified household head in that world did not run — running meant hitching up your robes and exposing your legs, a shameful, undignified thing. The father takes the shame that should have fallen on the returning son *onto himself*, publicly, before the son can even finish his rehearsed speech.
- Then the robe (the best one — the father's own honor), the ring (authority, restored sonship), the sandals (a son, not a servant — servants went barefoot), and the fatted calf (a feast for the whole village). This is not merely forgiveness. It is *full restoration to the household* — reinstatement as a son, celebrated publicly so the whole community must receive him back too.
- And the elder brother, refusing to go in to the feast, is committing his own public shaming of the father — and the father goes out and pleads with *him* too, taking on shame a second time. The story ends unresolved, hanging over the grumbling Pharisees: *will you come in to the feast, or not?*

**5. Location in the story.** This stands in the Gospels, at the arrival of the King who is gathering a covenant people from among the lost. It echoes the whole Bible's arc — a child of the household grasps for autonomy, ruins himself in a far country, and is restored not by earning his way back but by a father who bears the shame and welcomes him home. It is the covenant story of exile and homecoming, told in miniature, around a table.

**6. Crossing the bridge.** Only now, having heard what they heard, does it come home. Salvation here is not a legal transaction at a distance; it is a shamed child restored to the household — exactly the vision of adoption into God's family at the center of the Household-Covenant tradition. The father's running is the gospel: God taking our shame onto Himself to bring us home. And the unfinished ending asks the question of *us*: the feast is ready and the door is open — will you come in? Will your household be a place where the shamed are run to and restored at the table, or a place that grumbles at who is welcome?

That is one parable, walked. Notice how little of it required scholarship, and how much of it required only slowing down to ask what the first hearers already knew.

## Now you try

Pick a passage you think you already understand. Walk it through the six steps. Sit especially long on step 4 — the assumed world — because that is where the text most often has more to say than you remember. Do this at the table, with your household, and let the walk become a shared practice rather than a private study.

You will find that the passages you thought were flat were only unwalked.
