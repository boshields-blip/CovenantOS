# The Sacred and the Secular

---

> But you are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for his own possession, that you may proclaim the excellencies of him who called you out of darkness into his marvelous light. — 1 Peter 2:9

If [To See](/the-word/walking-the-text/to-see) is right — that the deepest failure between people is relating to a projected image instead of a received face — and if [The Two and the One](/the-word/walking-the-text/the-two-and-the-one) is right that the oldest place this failure shows itself is between man and woman, there is a second place nearly as old and nearly as costly: the line drawn between the ones who are called and the ones who are not. Ask most believers where ministry happens, and they will point to a stage, a title, a building with a steeple, a name on a bulletin. Ask them whether their own kitchen table, their own job site, their own street counts as a place of ministry, and most will hesitate — not because Scripture hesitates, but because centuries of habit have taught them to picture "ministry" as someone else's job, done somewhere else, by someone else's calling. That hesitation is the wound this page names.

## What the words used to mean

Begin with two words that have quietly reversed themselves.

*Laity* comes from the Greek *laos* — simply "people," and in the New Testament almost always **the whole people of God** (Titus 2:14; 1 Peter 2:9–10, echoing Exodus 19:5–6's "a people for his own possession"). *Laos* never meant the untrained, the passive, or the merely present. It named the whole covenant assembly — priest and farmer, mother and elder, alike — gathered as God's own. Somewhere on its long road into English, "the people of God" narrowed into "the people who are not really doing the ministry": a word built for the whole congregation was quietly repurposed as a word for its spectators.

*Clergy* runs the same road in reverse. It comes from *kleros* — "lot," or an allotted inheritance. Peter uses the word not for pastors ruling a congregation but for the congregation itself: elders are charged to shepherd "not domineering over those in your charge [*ton klērōn* — the portions, the flock allotted to you]" (1 Peter 5:3). The *kleros*, the inheritance, was the people — God's allotted possession, held in trust, never a rank to be occupied. Over the centuries the word migrated from naming the flock to naming the men set over it, until "clergy" came to mean nearly the opposite of what Peter used it for: not the whole people held in trust, but a class set apart from them.

Read in their own language, the two words that now build the wall were never made to build one. *Laos* was the whole people. *Kleros* was the whole people too, described twice, from two angles, as God's own possession. There was no wall in the vocabulary. It was built later, out of something other than the text — and then the words were reassigned to defend it.

## Naming the polarization

Hold that recovered vocabulary against how the modern church actually argues about calling, because — as with man and woman — most people feel trapped between two camps, and each one begins from a real wound.

**One pole reads ministry as office.** It says the called are a professional class — trained, credentialed, salaried, set apart by title and platform — and the rest of the church exists to be led, taught, and served by them. This camp is usually reacting to something true: doctrinal chaos, untrained voices doing real damage, congregations that drift wherever the loudest opinion blows. Its instinct to guard sound teaching and real accountability is not wrong. But its distortion is severe: it collapses calling into career, mutes the priesthood Peter addressed to the whole *laos*, and quietly teaches a congregation that their own ordinary lives are not where God is at work.

**The other pole reads calling as universal in a way that dissolves office.** It says every believer is equally a minister, therefore no one is set apart to teach, correct, or equip — that any recognized office is itself a power grab dressed in spiritual language. This camp, too, is reacting to something true: real spiritual abuse, celebrity collapse, congregations used as an audience for someone else's platform. Its protest against that is righteous. But its distortion is also severe: it dissolves the *kleros* Peter actually describes — an accountable people with some genuinely given to equip the rest (Ephesians 4:11–12) — and it hands calling over to the same [expressive individualism](/the-word/knowing-your-own-way/the-cultural-currents) that dissolves covenant everywhere else it touches: *my* calling, discerned alone, answerable to no one.

Here is the thing almost no one says: **both poles grew in the same soil.** Both are downstream of a church that had already turned ministry into either a hierarchy to be occupied or a platform to be built — a market with a stage and an audience. One camp keeps the hierarchy; the other tears it down and keeps the individualism underneath it. Neither has recovered the *kleros* — a whole people, genuinely one inheritance, within which some are genuinely given to equip the others for work that belongs to all of them. The whole argument is being conducted inside a broken frame. You do not have to win it. You can step behind it.

## What we are not saying

So let this be plain, because it is the point where people reach to sort you into a camp. This is **not** a claim that office, ordination, or a teaching gift are fictions invented to control people. Ephesians 4 is not embarrassed by the list — apostles, prophets, evangelists, pastors and teachers, genuinely given, genuinely different in gifting. And it is **not** a claim that every believer is authorized to teach with equal weight, unaccountable to anyone. Scripture gives neither a caste nor a formless crowd. It gives the *kleros*: one allotted people, genuinely equal in standing before God, within whom some are genuinely given — not to do the church's ministry *for* it, but "to equip the saints for the work of ministry" (Ephesians 4:12). The office exists to multiply the priesthood, not to replace it.

## Domination is the enemy, in either direction

And this must be said without hedging, because so many have been wounded here. **Domination is the sin the whole vision exists to heal — and it runs both ways.** A pastor who treats a congregation as his platform, his brand, his kleros to be managed rather than God's kleros held in trust, and a movement or a congregation that treats every recognized teaching gift as inherently suspect — silencing, discrediting, or refusing to submit to any correction at all — are doing the identical thing: trading a face for either a throne or a threat. Authority that dominates has already stopped being the office Peter describes; it has become the very thing he warned elders against in the same breath he gave them the charge. If you have been used by a pulpit, hear this clearly: that was not the calling Scripture teaches. It was its counterfeit. And if you have learned to treat every gifted teacher as a wolf until proven otherwise, hear this too: refusing all shepherding is not humility. It is its own kind of self-rule.

## The image at the pulpit, and the image in the pew

Most of this is not felt as theology. It is felt as two quieter failures of sight, and they mirror each other exactly. A congregation rarely sees the actual man behind a pulpit — his ordinary weaknesses, his private labor, his need for correction like any other believer — because a produced image has been built in his place: the stage, the lighting, the platform voice, the parasocial closeness of a screen that makes thousands feel they know someone they have never met. And a believer in the pew rarely sees their own kitchen table, their own trade, their own street as the actual site of their calling, because they are holding an *image* of what ministry looks like — a stage, a title, a building, a Sunday morning — and measuring their ordinary Tuesday against it and finding it wanting. Both are the same failure [To See](/the-word/walking-the-text/to-see) already named: relating to an image instead of receiving what is actually there. The cure is not a better platform or a flatter org chart. It is the same slow, humbling recovery of sight — setting down the image of what a minister looks like, and going looking for the actual, ordinary place God has already put you.

## An old wound, wearing new clothes

It is tempting to call this divide worse than the one Luther faced, and there is something true underneath the temptation, even if the claim needs care. Materially, it is hard to call anything worse than a laity barred from its own Scripture and sold assurance for money. But this version is harder to *see*, and that is its own kind of danger. The sixteenth-century wall was a formal doctrine, visible enough to name and nail to a door. This one hides behind the very language that was supposed to dissolve it — "every believer a minister," "the priesthood of all believers" — while functioning, in practice, through platforms, franchises, livestreams, and personal brands that concentrate visibility and authority in a very small number of people more thoroughly than a medieval parish ever could. Luther's fight tore down a wall you could point to. This one has to be seen before it can even be named, because it has learned to sound like the cure.

## Outward: the household's priesthood

This is why the home matters here too, and why this page belongs beside [The Ordered Household](/the-household/the-ordered-household) as much as beside its neighbor here. Before a believer ever discerns a calling to a title, they already hold one: the priesthood exercised at [Come to the Table](/the-word/living-the-covenant/come-to-the-table), where a father or mother reads Scripture aloud, prays over a meal, and forms children in the covenant — genuine priestly work, requiring no ordination and no stage, already commanded of the whole *laos*. A household that recovers this stops waiting for permission to minister and starts where the calling has been sitting the whole time.

And it does not stop at the door. A people who cannot see the priesthood at their own table will not see it in the shop, the field, or the street — the whole of ordinary life that [The Compact](/the-compact) takes up as public, covenantal ground. The recovery of the sacred and the secular is not, finally, a debate about church polity. It is where a whole culture of calling begins.
