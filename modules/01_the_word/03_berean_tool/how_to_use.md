# How to Use the Berean Tool

**Status:** Draft | **Author:** Brandon O'Shields

---

The Berean Tool is most useful as a **formation aid and conversation starter**, not as a verdict machine. It helps you name philosophical waters you are swimming in — for yourself, for a tradition, or for a teacher — so that you can compare them with the covenantal frame and respond with humility.

> **Start with yourself.** The most honest and fruitful use of this tool begins not with diagnosing someone else, but with examining your own inherited formation.

---

## Two modes of use

### Mode 1 — Self-assessment (recommended starting place)

Use the tool to ask: *What philosophies have shaped how I think about God, self, community, and hope?*

**Step 1 — Name your formation history**

Answer the background questions for yourself:

- What tradition(s) shaped my early faith?
- What schools, teachers, or books most formed my thinking?
- What phrases or metaphors do I reach for when talking about spiritual life, church, or identity?

**Step 2 — Work through the seven categories slowly**

For each category, ask the simple diagnostic question first:

| Category | Diagnostic question |
|---|---|
| Metaphysics | What do I treat as most real and most important day to day? |
| Epistemology | How do I decide whether something is true? What persuades me? |
| Anthropology | What do I actually think a person is for? What counts as flourishing? |
| Ethics | What language do I naturally use for the good? Holiness? Wellness? Impact? |
| Social/Political | How do I imagine the community of faith — brand, family, movement, covenant? |
| Identity/Meaning | Where do I locate the "real me" — in my choices, my story, my wounds, my calling? |
| Eschatology | What future story makes sense of my present obedience? |

**Step 3 — Notice, don't prosecute**

The goal is not to produce a list of personal failures. It is to notice patterns and ask where they came from. Some will be covenantal inheritances; others will be cultural loans. Many are a blend.

**Step 4 — Compare with the covenantal frame**

For each category, read the corresponding covenantal contrast statement in [02_diagnostic_engine/categories](../02_diagnostic_engine/categories/). Ask: *Where does my instinctive answer differ from this covenantal answer? Why?*

**Step 5 — Receive a formation recommendation**

The final step is not critique but invitation. Each category that shows significant drift suggests a corresponding formation practice — not condemnation, but a path toward a more covenantal way of seeing.

---

### Mode 2 — Teacher analysis (secondary use)

Only after some self-assessment is it productive to apply the tool to teachers, pastors, or traditions you are learning from. The reason for this ordering is simple: without self-awareness, analysis of others easily becomes self-protective or tribal.

**Steps for teacher analysis:**

1. Gather public information — do not speculate about private motives, character, or sincerity.
2. Identify the tradition and educational lineage using [tradition_influence_map.md](./tradition_influence_map.md).
3. Work through the seven categories using what you have actually heard or read, not caricatures.
4. Notice strengths first, then drifts.
5. Use the [berean_profile_template.md](./berean_profile_template.md) to write up your analysis in a structured, balanced way.
6. End with "What can I receive from this teacher?" rather than a verdict.

---

## Reflection questions for each mode

These questions are designed to slow down the analysis and encourage generous inquiry.

### For self-assessment

1. Which category felt most uncomfortable to answer honestly? Why?
2. Which inherited philosophy has brought me real goods that I should not discard?
3. What phrase or metaphor do I use that I'd now examine more carefully?
4. Who in my tradition has modeled a more covenantal form of this than I have received?
5. What would it look like to adopt a more covenantal posture in this category — practically, not just conceptually?

### For teacher analysis

1. What genuine good has this teacher's formation produced — what is nourishing, faithful, or wise in what they offer?
2. Where do I see the strengths of their tradition coming through?
3. Where do I see the pressures of their tradition creating blind spots?
4. Is there a category where this teacher is more covenantal than their institution — where they have exceeded or resisted inherited drifts?
5. What would I say to this teacher charitably — not as a corrector, but as a fellow learner?

---

## A note on charitable analysis

The Berean Tool is not a tribunal. It is a map.

Maps help you navigate, not condemn the terrain. A pastor who leans toward therapeutic language in their anthropology has not necessarily abandoned faithfulness; they may be responding to real wounds, using an inherited vocabulary that needs deepening rather than dismissal.

The goal of using the tool charitably is to say: *I can appreciate what this stream offers, I can name where it tends to drift, and I can receive what is good while growing in discernment about what needs reform.*

## Small-group use

The Berean Tool works well for slow group conversations. Suggested format for a small-group session:

1. **Open (10 minutes):** Each person names one tradition or teacher that significantly shaped them, and something they genuinely appreciate about that influence.
2. **Category focus (20 minutes):** Choose one of the seven categories. Read its diagnostic questions aloud. Each person shares: *What was I taught about this? What do I actually believe? Are those the same?*
3. **Honest comparison (10 minutes):** Read the covenantal answer for that category from [covenantal_philosophy.md](../01_foundational_layer/covenantal_philosophy.md). Ask: *What draws you toward this contrast? What resists it?*
4. **Formation invitation (10 minutes):** What one small change in language, practice, or posture would move someone toward a more covenantal pattern in this category?
5. **Close:** Pray or read a Scripture passage that speaks to the category you studied.

---

## How to read a completed Berean profile

When you read a completed profile — whether for yourself or a teacher — read in this order:

1. **Strengths first.** What gifts does this formation bring? What is genuinely trustworthy here?
2. **Influences second.** What streams shaped this? Where do those streams come from historically?
3. **Drifts third.** Where do those streams tend to pull away from covenantal clarity?
4. **Contrasts fourth.** What would a more covenantal pattern look like in these categories?
5. **Formation invitation last.** What specific practice, posture, or vocabulary shift might help?

This ordering reflects the conviction that discernment serves formation, and formation is not primarily self-correction but grateful growth.
