# Host Guide — Come to the Table

**Status:** Draft | **Author:** Brandon O'Shields

---

## Who this guide is for

This guide is for the parent, guardian, or adult who leads the weekly table practice. You do not need to be a pastor, a theologian, or a trained Bible teacher. You need to be willing to open a Bible, read a short passage, and ask simple questions with patience.

---

## How to prepare in ten minutes

The session files in `sessions/` are designed so that a host can prepare in a single short read. Here is how:

1. **Read the session file once.** Note the passage reference, the author, the Know slot category, the notice prompts, and the live invitation.
2. **Open your Bible to the passage.** Read it once to yourself. You do not need to understand it fully before the session — your job is to read it aloud, not to explain it.
3. **Choose the Know slot point.** The session file will tell you which category is up (author, audience, language, genre, frame, philosophy, or tradition). Read the corresponding entry in [AUTHORS_AND_LANGUAGES.md](/the-word/living-the-covenant/come-to-the-table/authors-and-languages).
4. **Glance at the notice prompts.** Each session file has one prompt per age band. You will not read these aloud — they are cues to help you invite each age naturally in the *Notice* movement.
5. **Know the live invitation.** You will say this once, near the end. It should be concrete and short enough that you can repeat it without looking at notes.

That is all. Put the device away. Come to the table.

## How to read aloud well

Reading aloud is a skill, and it improves quickly with practice. A few principles:

**Read slowly.** Most people read aloud faster than they think. A short passage read slowly is more valuable than a longer passage read quickly.

**Pause at natural breaks.** After a sentence. After a list. After a verse that names something important. Let the words land.

**Do not explain as you read.** Read the passage first; talk about it afterward. If you stop to explain every phrase, the passage never has a chance to speak for itself.

**If you stumble on a word or name, say it anyway.** Hebrew and Greek names are unfamiliar; stumbling is not a failure. The household sees a person trying, not an expert performing.

**Read it twice when young children are present.** The first reading is for the older bands. The second reading allows younger children a second pass, which is often when a single image or word catches.

See [liturgies/reading.md](/the-word/living-the-covenant/come-to-the-table/liturgies/reading) for more guidance.

## How to ask "what did you hear?" without turning it into a quiz

*Notice* is the movement that most easily slides into performance pressure. A few ways to keep it from becoming a quiz:

**Ask what they heard, not what it means.** "What did you hear?" invites observation. "What does this mean?" invites performance. Start with the first.

**Welcome all answers.** If a child says something that has nothing to do with the passage, receive it. "That's interesting — what made you think of that?" is not a redirect; it is a genuine question.

**Do not supply the answer you are looking for.** If you ask "what did you hear?" and no one says the thing you were hoping to hear, let it go. The passage offered them what it offered them.

**Do not correct.** If a child says something factually wrong about the passage — "Moses was an Egyptian king" — do not say "no, that's not right." Say "what made you think of Moses?" and let the session move.

**Keep the round moving.** The goal is one turn per person, not a thorough discussion. After everyone has spoken once, you may invite a second round — but the discipline of one-turn-first is important.

## How to handle silence

Silence is not failure. Some of the most important formation happens in silence.

Wait five to ten seconds before re-inviting. If the youngest child is silent, re-invite very gently: *"[Name], is there a word you heard? Just one word is fine."* If an older child or adult is silent, let the silence stand a moment longer.

Silence at the table often means something is being processed. Silence after a short passage of Scripture is not awkward; it is appropriate.

---

## How to handle wrong answers

See *Notice* guidance above. The short version: welcome wrong answers as though they are observations worth exploring. They usually are.

The exception is if a wrong answer might hurt another person at the table. If a child says something dismissive or unkind about another person in response to a passage, name that gently and redirect.

---

## How to handle tangents

Tangents are mostly good. They mean the passage touched something real. The host's job is not to eliminate tangents but to keep them from consuming the whole session.

A reliable way to close a tangent: *"That's worth thinking about more. Let me add one thing from the passage and then we'll get to Live."* Then do exactly that. You do not need to resolve the tangent tonight.

---

## How to keep it inside ~fifteen minutes

The session will rarely go long if:
- The passage is short (one to five verses is usually enough).
- *Notice* moves one turn per person and does not loop back multiple times.
- *Know* is genuinely one thing, said briefly.
- *Live* names one invitation and closes.

If you find the sessions consistently running long, the most likely cause is that *Notice* is becoming a discussion instead of a round. A round means every person speaks once before anyone speaks twice.

---

## How to involve the youngest first

Begin every *Notice* round with the youngest person present. Not because their answer will be the most insightful — sometimes it will be, and sometimes it will be a non sequitur. Begin with them because:

- It signals that the youngest belongs here.
- It creates a habit that older bands learn to honor.
- It often generates surprising warmth — an older sibling will frequently affirm or build on what the youngest said.

The simplest prompt for a young child is: *"[Name], what's a word you heard?"* Any word is fine. Receive it.

---

## How to close

The closing blessing in [liturgies/closing.md](/the-word/living-the-covenant/come-to-the-table/liturgies/closing) is brief — meant to be said together, if possible. After the blessing, the session is done. The household returns to the rest of the meal or the work of clearing plates.

Resist the urge to add a summary or a lesson after the closing. The closing is the closing. Let it end.

---

## When it feels flat

Some sessions will feel flat. The passage won't land. The kids will be distracted. The conversation will stall. The live invitation will seem abstract.

That is normal. The practice has value that does not depend on any single session feeling formative. A household that shows up to a flat session is still a household that shows up. That faithfulness matters.
