# Canonical Arc — Come to the Table

**Status:** Draft | **Author:** Brandon O'Shields

---

## What this arc is

The canonical arc is a multi-year, low-pressure path through the Scriptures designed for one session per week. At roughly fifty sessions per year, a household following this arc will move through a meaningful portion of the Bible over four years without turning the table into a completion project.

The arc is a path, not a treadmill. Missed weeks are fine. Returning to a passage you've already covered is fine. The arc does not punish absence or require perfection. It is simply a way to avoid drift and to ensure that, over time, the household hears the whole story rather than a favorite corner of it.

---

## The four-year arc

### Year 1 — Who God is

**Theme:** *The character of God, established before any human story is told.*

Year 1 begins at the beginning. The goal is simple: by the end of the year, every person at the table can say something true and particular about who God is — not in abstract terms, but from specific passages they have heard.

**Suggested movements:**
- Genesis 1–12 — creation, covenant, human failure, first promises
- Selected Psalms — praise, lament, trust, the throne of God
- The Gospel of Mark — Jesus in action, signs, confrontations, the cross
- Selected Exodus scenes — the burning bush, the plagues, the Red Sea crossing, Sinai, the tabernacle

Roughly fifty sessions spread across these texts gives the household a sense of God as Creator, God as covenant-keeper, God as Redeemer, and God as present.

### Year 2 — The covenant story

**Theme:** *The spine of the story from Abraham to the church.*

Year 2 follows the central narrative arc of Scripture: the covenant made with Abraham, traced through Moses, David, the Prophets, and fulfilled in Jesus, continuing in the early church. The goal is not exhaustive coverage but a living sense of the story's backbone.

**Suggested movements:**
- Abraham — the call, the promise, the test (Genesis 12–22, selected)
- Moses — the exodus, the law, the failure, the grace (Exodus, Numbers, Deuteronomy, selected)
- David — the king, the covenant, the fall, the Psalms (1–2 Samuel, Psalms, selected)
- The Prophets — Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel (one passage each, selected scenes of judgment and hope)
- Jesus — the Sermon on the Mount, the parables, the passion (Matthew, selected)
- The Church — Acts, selected letters, glimpses of what the first communities were like

One passage per week along the spine of the covenant story. By the end, the household has a skeleton of the whole biblical narrative in living memory.

### Year 3 — The voices

**Theme:** *The human authors of Scripture as distinct persons.*

Year 3 rotates through the major human voices of Scripture. The goal is that the household knows *whose* voice is whose — that Moses sounds different from David, that Isaiah sounds different from Luke, that Paul sounds different from John.

**Suggested rotation:**
- Moses — select passages from Genesis, Exodus, and Deuteronomy
- David — select Psalms
- Isaiah — select passages from chapters 1–12, 40–55, 56–66
- Luke — select passages from Luke and Acts
- Paul — select passages from Romans, Galatians, and Philippians
- John — select passages from John, 1 John, and Revelation
- Peter — select passages from 1 and 2 Peter
- James — select passages from James

Each author gets several sessions. By the end of Year 3, the household can hear a passage they have not read before and make a reasonable guess at whose voice is speaking.

### Year 4 — The philosophy

**Theme:** *Scripture read explicitly through the seven covenantal categories.*

Year 4 is the most intellectually demanding year, and it should feel like a natural arrival rather than a curriculum shift. The household has now heard a great deal of Scripture. Year 4 asks: *what kind of world does Scripture say we are in? what kind of beings are we? how is truth known? where is history going?*

The Know slot's *Philosophy point* category has been quietly laying the groundwork since Year 1. Year 4 makes it explicit.

**Suggested structure — seven passages for each category:**
1. **Metaphysics** — what is real; what God has made; where creation comes from and where it is going
2. **Epistemology** — how God makes Himself known; how faith and reason relate; the role of the community in discernment
3. **Anthropology** — what a human being is; image-bearing; the body; vocation; failure and restoration
4. **Ethics** — what goodness looks like; the law and the gospel; holiness and love; justice and mercy
5. **Social/Political** — how communities are ordered; authority and accountability; family, congregation, civil society
6. **Identity/Meaning** — where identity is grounded; vocation and calling; belonging to God's people
7. **Eschatology** — where history is going; resurrection and judgment; the hope that shapes present faithfulness

Seven passages for each category, at one session per week, brings Year 4 to roughly forty-nine sessions — close enough to a year.

---

## Starting over

After Year 4, a household can begin Year 1 again. The passages will be familiar; the people at the table will be older. What an eight-year-old heard in Year 1 will land differently when they are twelve; what a parent noticed in Year 1 may surface differently after four more years of practice.

The arc is designed to be repeated. Each pass through deepens rather than duplicates.

---

## Starting anywhere

A household does not need to start at Year 1, Session 1. They can begin wherever a passage calls to them. They can start at Year 2 if the Psalms feel too unfamiliar. They can run Year 3 if they have older children already. They can also ignore the arc entirely for a time and simply keep a faithful table.

---

## What this arc does over time

A household that follows this arc for eight to ten years will have heard much of the Old and New Testament read aloud at the table. They will have encountered the major authors, languages, genres, covenant movements, and philosophical categories not as abstractions but as accumulated memory.

That is not a curriculum. It is formation — gradual, embodied, relational, and covenantal.
