# Sin, Sacrifice, and Righteousness

---

> For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God. — 2 Corinthians 5:21

Covenantal Worship traces what the five offerings answer. This page traces something underneath that: what sin actually breaks, what a sacrifice actually does about it, and what it means to become righteous. Get this wrong and the cross shrinks to a transaction — a debt paid, a ledger balanced, a substance moved from one column to another. Get it right and the whole story reads as one continuous act of repair: a broken relationship and a distorted vocation, restored through a representative, into a life we now share rather than a status we merely hold.

## What sin actually breaks

Sin is not a toxin that gets on you or passes between people like a contagion. It is a breach — of relationship first, and of vocation right alongside it. Genesis places humanity in the world with a calling: image-bearers, given dominion, told to "work it and keep it" (Genesis 1:26–28; 2:15) — the same two verbs Israel's priests will later use for their service in the tabernacle. Humanity's first job was priestly before it was anything else: to tend a sacred space in the presence of God.

Genesis 3 is the story of that job and that presence both failing at once. Adam and Eve do not simply break a rule; they grasp for a knowledge that was never theirs to seize, and the result is immediate on both fronts — hiding from God's presence, and then exile from the garden they were placed there to keep (Genesis 3:8, 23–24). That is the biblical shape of sin from the very first pages: a relationship ruptured and a calling abandoned, together. Whatever repair looks like, it has to address both, or it has not actually addressed sin.

## What a sacrifice actually does: representation, not transfer

Leviticus never describes sin moving from a person into an animal. What it describes is a worshiper laying a hand on the animal's head (Leviticus 1:4) — a gesture of identification, the same kind of gesture used elsewhere in Scripture to ordain, commission, or install someone in another's place (Numbers 27:18–23). The animal is not a sponge. It is a stand-in, appointed to bear what the worshiper cannot bear for himself and to do, in his place, what needs doing to restore access to God.

The clearest picture of this is the scapegoat on the Day of Atonement. Aaron lays both hands on the goat's head, confesses Israel's sins over it by name, and the goat "shall bear on itself all their iniquities to a remote area" (Leviticus 16:21–22). The text is careful with its verb: the goat *bears*. It does not *absorb*. Bearing is what a representative does with a real burden — carrying it away, taking the consequence, so the one it stands for does not have to. Even the priest's own role is described this way: he is "given... to bear the iniquity of the congregation, to make atonement for them" (Leviticus 10:17). This is representational burden-bearing, not a metaphysical exchange, and Isaiah keeps the same vocabulary when it describes the Servant: he "has borne our griefs and carried our sorrows," and "the LORD has laid on him the iniquity of us all" (Isaiah 53:4–6).

## "He became sin": reading Paul through Leviticus

Paul's language about Jesus is drawn straight from this well. "For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God" (2 Corinthians 5:21) does not mean Jesus became sinful, or that sin was poured into him like a liquid. Many readers of this verse hear it against the same Levitical background: the Greek word for "sin," *hamartia*, is also the word the Greek Old Testament uses again and again for the sin offering itself. Paul does something similar with the same vocabulary in Romans, writing that God sent his Son "for sin" — the Greek there, *peri hamartias*, is the Septuagint's standing technical phrase for a sin offering — "he condemned sin in the flesh" (Romans 8:3). Read this way, Paul is not saying God made Jesus sinful. He is saying God made Jesus the offering, in the fullest Levitical sense of that word: the appointed representative who bears the consequence and performs the repair.

This is not a way of softening what the cross accomplished. Isaiah is explicit that the Servant's suffering is real substitution — "he was pierced for our transgressions... upon him was the chastisement that brought us peace" (Isaiah 53:5) — and Peter says plainly that "he himself bore our sins in his body on the tree" (1 Peter 2:24). Something is genuinely borne, genuinely carried, genuinely absorbed at real cost. The correction is narrower than it sounds: not "the cross accomplished less than we thought," but "the mechanism is representation and bearing, the same categories Leviticus already gave us, not a mystical transfer of substance that Leviticus never describes and Scripture never asks us to picture."

## Adam and Christ: two representative heads

Scripture applies this same representative logic at the scale of the whole human family, not only at the altar. Romans 5 sets two men against each other as heads whose actions reach everyone joined to them: "as by the one man's disobedience the many were made sinners, so by the one man's obedience the many will be made righteous" (Romans 5:19). This is not Adam's sin leaking into his descendants like a genetic stain, any more than it is our sin being poured into Christ. It is covenantal headship: what the representative does, his people inherit — the pattern, the consequence, the standing — because they are joined to him. Adam's fall distorts the human vocation for everyone born into his line. Christ's obedience restores it for everyone joined to his.

## Righteousness: reckoned and shared, not deposited

If sin is not a substance, righteousness is not one either. It is not a status sticker God affixes to an otherwise unchanged person, and it is not a stockpile transferred from Christ's account into ours. Scripture's own word for what happens is "reckoned" — Abraham "believed the LORD, and he counted it to him as righteousness" (Genesis 15:6), and Paul builds a whole argument in Romans 4 on that same verb, closing with the reason it was written down: not for Abraham's sake alone, but for ours, "who believe in him who raised from the dead Jesus our Lord... who was raised for our justification" (Romans 4:23–25).

Reckoning is a relational verdict, not a substance transfer, and Paul is just as insistent that this verdict comes bundled with an actual union. "Christ Jesus... became to us wisdom from God, righteousness and sanctification and redemption" (1 Corinthians 1:30) — the righteousness is his, and it becomes ours because we are joined to him, the way a branch shares the life of the vine rather than receiving a shipment from it. "I have been crucified with Christ," Paul says. "It is no longer I who live, but Christ who lives in me" (Galatians 2:20). Baptism pictures the same thing: buried with him, raised with him, so that we might "walk in newness of life" (Romans 6:4). We are not made righteous the way a check is deposited. We are made righteous the way a person becomes part of a family — by real, effective union with the one whose standing and whose life we now share.

## Vocation restored, not only a verdict changed

Because the original breach was vocational as well as relational, the repair has to be too. Paul calls believers "his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand, that we should walk in them" (Ephesians 2:10) — the priestly calling of Genesis 2, given back. The "new self" being put on is being "renewed in knowledge after the image of its creator" (Colossians 3:10) — the image-bearing vocation of Genesis 1, restored, not merely forgiven. A gospel that only changes your legal status and leaves your calling untouched has told half the story. The other half is that Jesus is the last Adam: not only the one who bears what Adam's line owed, but the one who begins, in his own restored humanity, the vocation Adam's line abandoned.

## Generational brokenness: real patterns, not a mystical stain

This is also the right frame for the brokenness that runs in families across generations. Scripture does speak of consequences reaching to the third and fourth generation (Exodus 34:6–7) — but in the very next breath insists this is not guilt inherited like an heirloom: "the soul who sins shall die. The son shall not suffer for the iniquity of the father" (Ezekiel 18:20). What passes from one generation to the next is pattern, not culpability — a household's habits, wounds, and blind spots, learned and re-enacted long before they are ever chosen. Nobody stands guilty before God for what a parent or grandparent did. But everybody inherits the shape of the household that raised them, for better and for worse, and that shape gets handed on again unless someone in the line does the harder work of naming it. This is exactly the ground the Ordered Household's own word on generational faithfulness stands on: building honestly on what was invested, while having the honest conversations that keep a pattern from simply repeating itself unexamined.

## Putting it together

| | What it names | What actually happens | Where it lands |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sin | A breach — of relationship with God, of vocation in the world | Real damage, real consequence, real distance | Hiding, exile, distorted calling |
| Sacrifice | Representation — a substitute bears the consequence and performs the repair | Real bearing, real cost, real access restored | Christ, the once-for-all offering |
| Righteousness | Reckoning and union — a verdict declared because a life is shared | Real standing, real belonging, real vocation | Adoption into Christ's own righteous life |

None of this is a smaller gospel than "Jesus paid the price for your sin." It is that claim, held together with the two things a purely transactional telling tends to lose: that what was broken was a relationship and a calling, not only a balance sheet, and that what is given back is not a status alone but a shared life, out of which relationship and calling both start growing again.

## A note on how tightly to hold this

The language of "transfer" — sin transferred to Christ, righteousness transferred to us — is old, common, and not without support; the "great exchange" has been preached faithfully for centuries and points at something true. What this page argues is narrower: that "transfer" is a metaphor Scripture itself does not use for either sin or righteousness, and that the actual biblical vocabulary — bearing, representing, reckoning, being united to — carries the same weight without asking us to picture sin as a substance that moves. Faithful readers who prefer the exchange language and faithful readers who prefer this page's vocabulary are usually confessing the very same realities: real substitution, real imputation, real union. Hold the categories above firmly enough to let them shape how you read Leviticus and Romans together, loosely enough to keep listening to sisters and brothers who reach for the older words to say the same thing.
