# Covenantal Worship

---

> For it is impossible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins. Consequently, when Christ came into the world, he said, "Sacrifices and offerings you have not desired, but a body have you prepared for me... Then I said, 'Behold, I have come to do your will, O God.'" — Hebrews 10:4–7

I have been reading through Exodus and Leviticus this season, and one thing keeps surfacing. Each covenant has its own shape of worship, tuned to its own moment in the story — Noah's altar is not Sinai's tabernacle, and Sinai's tabernacle is not the table where the church breaks bread on a Sunday. God meets each covenant people where they actually are. But underneath the different shapes, the same threads keep running through: an altar, blood, a meal, a word spoken over it all. Worship changes its clothes from covenant to covenant. It does not change its bones.

We do not sacrifice animals to worship Jesus, and nobody is arguing that we should. But I think we lose something real when that true fact quietly shrinks into a smaller one: that Jesus's death was "a payment for sin and guilt" and nothing more. That is one of the truest things Scripture says about the cross. It is not the only true thing. Leviticus never lets a single offering carry the whole weight of worship by itself — it gives Israel five, each doing different work, each naming a different dimension of what it means to belong to God. If we let "payment for guilt" become the only lens we bring to the cross, we do not get a false Christ. We get a true Christ, reduced to one movement of a much larger piece of music.

## Five offerings, not one flattened sacrifice

Leviticus 1–7 lays out five kinds of offering, and a sixth element — a drink offering of wine — that is poured out *alongside* the others rather than standing as its own independent sacrifice (see Numbers 15:1–10). Each one answers a different question a worshiper might bring to the altar: What do I owe with my whole self? What do I owe from my ordinary labor? What has come between me and the God I need access to? What have I damaged that needs repair? And where do I actually get to sit down and eat with Him?

### 1. The burnt offering (ʿōlāh) — total belonging

The burnt offering is wholly consumed. Nothing is kept back by priest or worshiper; the whole animal ascends as smoke (Leviticus 1). Noah's first act after the flood is this offering, and God receives it as "a pleasing aroma" (Genesis 8:20–21) — the aroma, in other words, of a gift held back not at all. This is the offering of total consecration: not *I owe you something*, but *I belong to you entirely*.

Jesus fulfills this dimension by being the offering himself. Hebrews reads Psalm 40 as Christ's own words at his coming: "sacrifices and offerings you have not desired... then I said, 'Behold, I have come to do your will'" (Hebrews 10:5–7) — obedience offered whole, not partial. Paul calls Christ's self-giving "a fragrant offering" to God (Ephesians 5:2), using the burnt offering's own vocabulary. And this dimension becomes the shape of ordinary Christian life: "present your bodies as a living sacrifice" (Romans 12:1). Baptism is the entry into that same total belonging — not a transaction, a consecration.

### 2. The grain offering (minḥāh) — the gift of ordinary life, with joy poured out alongside it

The grain offering is flour, oil, and frankincense — the produce of daily labor, salted as a sign of covenant loyalty, a portion burned and the rest given to the priests to eat (Leviticus 2). It is worship made of bread and work rather than blood. The drink offering usually rides alongside it: wine poured out, not kept, the taste of covenant joy rather than covenant debt.

Jesus takes up both threads. He is "the bread of life" (John 6:35) and "the firstfruits" of a harvest still coming (1 Corinthians 15:20) — the grain offering's whole logic, fulfilled in a person. And at his last meal, he takes the cup and calls it "my blood of the covenant, poured out" (Matthew 26:27–28), then tells the disciples he will drink it new with them in his Father's kingdom (Matthew 26:29) — the drink offering's joy, deferred but not denied. In worship, this becomes the offertory, the daily prayer for bread, and the plain conviction that ordinary work — the trade, the harvest, the meal made from scratch — is itself an offering, not merely the thing that funds the "real" worship happening somewhere else.

### 3. The purification offering (ḥaṭṭāʾt) — cleansing what sin has contaminated

This one is usually translated "sin offering," and the label is part of why the whole system gets flattened. Its actual concern is not a guilty conscience; it is contaminated sacred space. Blood is applied to the altar or the sanctuary itself to decontaminate what impurity and unintentional wrong have defiled (Leviticus 4) — the problem is relational and spatial before it is psychological. Something has come between the people and the place where God meets them, and it has to be cleaned before access is possible again.

Hebrews says Christ's blood does this at the true scale: it purifies not a tent but "the heavenly things themselves" (Hebrews 9:23), clearing the way into God's actual presence rather than a copy of it. In worship, this becomes confession — not groveling, but the honest naming of what has come between us and God, followed by the assurance that the way back in is actually open. Some traditions formalize this moment with a spoken word of pardon (what liturgical traditions call absolution); most of this corpus's own practice keeps it simpler — confession offered, forgiveness received, the table set again.

### 4. The guilt offering (ʾāšām) — what is owed gets repaid

This offering is about restitution, not merely pardon. Where something has actually been damaged — a debt, a trespass, an encroachment on what belongs to God or to a neighbor — the worshiper repays what was taken, adds a fifth to it, and then brings a ram (Leviticus 5–6). This is the offering of justice actually restored, not just excused.

Isaiah names the Servant's death using this exact word: "when his soul makes an offering for guilt [ʾāšām], he shall see his offspring" (Isaiah 53:10). This is the most precise of all the offering-to-Christ connections in Scripture — not a loose analogy, the same Hebrew term the Torah uses for repayment-plus-restitution, applied directly to what the cross accomplishes. This is the offering that most naturally carries the "payment" language, and it is real: something genuinely owed is genuinely repaid, not waived. But it is one offering among five, not the whole system standing in for itself. In worship, it becomes reconciliation and, where it is possible, actual restitution — not only saying sorry, but repairing what was broken, between people and not only before God.

### 5. The peace offering (šĕlāmîm) — the meal at the center

Of the five, this is the only one built around eating. The fat goes to God, a portion goes to the priests, and the worshiper's own household eats the rest in God's presence (Leviticus 3; Leviticus 7:11–21, which names three kinds — thanksgiving, vow, and freewill). It is a shared table with three parties at it, and it is the offering Israel brought most often, because most of worship is not crisis. It is fellowship.

Jesus fulfills this one by hosting it. At the table on the night he was betrayed, he is both the host and the meal — "this is my body, given for you" (Luke 22:19–20) — and every meal his followers keep in his name afterward carries that same three-way shape: God, the gathered household, and the food shared between them. Some traditions call this table the Eucharist; this project has always called it simply the Table. Whatever the name, this is the offering that worship keeps coming back to, the one none of the others can substitute for, because it is the one that actually seats you at the meal instead of only clearing the way to it.

## Putting it together

| Offering | Hebrew | What it answers | Fulfilled in Jesus | Fulfilled in worship |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Burnt | *ʿōlāh* | What do I owe with my whole self? | Total obedience (Hebrews 10:5–7) | Self-offering, baptism (Romans 12:1) |
| Grain, with the drink offering | *minḥāh* | What do I owe from my ordinary labor and joy? | Bread of life, firstfruits, new wine (John 6:35; 1 Cor 15:20; Matt 26:29) | Offertory, daily bread, sanctified work |
| Purification | *ḥaṭṭāʾt* | What has come between me and God's presence? | Purifies the true sanctuary (Hebrews 9:23) | Confession, cleansed access |
| Guilt / reparation | *ʾāšām* | What is owed, and what needs repaying? | Bears our guilt, repays what we could not (Isaiah 53:10) | Reconciliation, restitution |
| Peace | *šĕlāmîm* | Where do I get to sit and eat with God? | Hosts the table, is the meal (Luke 22:19–20) | The Table, ongoing fellowship |

Worship this wide is not a chart to master. It is a way of noticing that the cross is doing more at once than any single word can hold — total surrender, ordinary gratitude, real cleansing, real repayment, and a meal that is still being eaten. Reduce it to one offering and you have not made the gospel simpler. You have made it thinner.

## Sacred space, not a summit to earn

Here is the thing the five offerings were building toward all along, and it is easy to miss if you only watch the mechanics: the goal was never merely clearing an account. Repentance is real, and a clear conscience matters — the purification and guilt offerings above are not optional or symbolic. But they were always the path, not the destination. The destination was the meal. The whole system exists so that God and his people can actually be in the same place together.

I think the clearest way to say this is with a picture rather than an argument. If you take your children to the rim of the Grand Canyon, they do not need to climb down into it to have actually been there. If you take them to the foot of Everest, they do not need to summit it to stand in real awe of it. The people who do descend into the canyon or ascend the mountain do not begin anywhere different than everyone else — they begin the same way your children do, in awe of something too large for them, before they ever take a step. Awe is not the consolation prize for those who cannot make the climb. It is the actual point, available to everyone at the rim.

Scripture pictures the mountain of God's presence the same way. At Sinai, Israel is not invited to climb — they are told to keep their distance and not so much as touch the mountain's edge, on pain of death, while Moses alone goes up on their behalf (Exodus 19:12–13). The psalmist asks the question everyone standing at the base is really asking — "Who shall ascend the hill of the LORD? And who shall stand in his holy place?" — and answers it with the same clean-hands, pure-heart language the purification and guilt offerings exist to supply (Psalm 24:3–4). And when Jacob wakes from his dream of a ladder set up between heaven and earth, he does not try to climb it. He is simply undone by where he is standing: "How awesome is this place! This is none other than the house of God, and this is the gate of heaven" (Genesis 28:16–17).

Jesus does not send us up that ladder. He becomes it. He tells Nathanael that he himself is the place where heaven opens and the traffic between heaven and earth — Jacob's angels, ascending and descending — now runs (John 1:51). He is the one who actually goes down into the depths and up to the summit, "descended into the lower regions" and then "ascended far above all the heavens, that he might fill all things" (Ephesians 4:8–10) — not so we could watch him do it, but so the whole space between could finally be filled, top to bottom, with the presence he opened it to. And because he has already gone in ahead of us, "a forerunner on our behalf" (Hebrews 6:19–20), we get to draw near — not by our own climbing, but by his — "with confidence... that we may receive mercy and find grace to help in time of need" (Hebrews 4:14–16).

That is why worship was never meant to be a descent into your own psychology, some private excavation of your inner life, or an ascent you have to perform to earn your way into God's presence. It is neither a plunge into yourself nor a climb you make alone. God is the one who moved — down into the world at Bethlehem, up again at the ascension — and the sacred space he opened by moving is the space every offering in Leviticus was quietly pointing toward, and the space every Table since has been set inside of. You do not have to scale the canyon wall. You only have to come stand at the rim, and be amazed at who is already there.

## A note on how tightly to hold this

The New Testament draws real, explicit lines between the sacrificial system and Christ — Hebrews does it directly for the purification offering, Isaiah does it by name for the guilt offering. It does not, anywhere, hand us a finished five-column chart and tell us this is the only correct way to map it. The correspondences above are well-attested and, I think, genuinely illuminating, but they are a teaching arrangement laid over the text to help us see something true, not a grid Scripture itself printed. Hold it the way you would hold any good typology: firmly enough to let it change how you read Leviticus, loosely enough to keep listening if the text itself pushes back on a detail.
