# Lifted Up

---

> A note before you read this. Depression is real, and it can be dangerous — this poem says so plainly: "many... drown in self-despair." If you are in a season like that right now, please do not read what follows as this project's word on what to do with it. Talk to someone — a pastor, a counselor, a doctor, someone who actually knows you — and if you are ever in danger of harming yourself, call or text 988 (in the US) or reach out to someone who can sit with you in person. This page is not that help. It is a poem.
>
> What this poem is actually about is a different, quieter danger: mistaking the examined life for the saved life, and diving into yourself — your own wisdom, your own depth, your own moral seriousness — as if going deep enough into *you* could ever be the same as being found by God. The "dying" in it is not a wish to not exist. It is what Scripture calls dying to self: letting go of pride, of self-reliance, of the exhausting work of justifying and saving yourself, the way Jesus described losing your life in order to actually find it (Matthew 16:25). Like the lament that precedes this one, it starts in the dark and stays there a while before it turns. Read it slowly. Let the turn do its work.

---

I see all men as men destined for water. We must all eventually embrace the chaos of the sea and its depths, but most choose the security of the surface and live life in the shallows of self-preservation.

The sea is full of beasts, and many are swallowed up by the creatures of the deep or drown in self-despair.

The world embraces life upon the surface. It builds its floating cities and travels on its ships.

Safety and security drive people to raise greater towers of Babel to keep them safe from the waters. Self-preservation is their moral compass, and the common good is only the name they give their shared fear.

In my pride and my desire not to conform, I train myself to swim and dive deep. I do not want to live in a superficial reality when I am a man destined for water. I want to embrace the water and understand its depths.

The more I dive, the more I want to separate myself from this world with my deeper knowledge and my wisdom.

As I dive deep, I see the vanities of life but find myself with no joy. I can no longer enjoy the simple boat rides and the simple life upon the floating cities. I refuse to be like the men of the surface, for they are shallow and have no depth — but I envy them, because they can still enjoy simple pleasures.

Would life have been more enjoyable in ignorance and conformity? The more I dive and hold my breath, the more I feel as though my body cannot contain the pressures of the deep.

My breath escapes me, and I feel as though my mind will implode from the pressure of diving into the depths of unfathomable realities.

Perhaps life would have been easier in the shallows of my own self-preservation, but that shallow life no longer satisfies me.

Why should I keep fighting to stay afloat on my own strength? I am tired of self-preservation. Life on the surface is shallow, and I do not fit within man-made and artificial realities. So I stop swimming. I let the weight of everything I have been carrying — my pride, my need to be right, my need to be enough on my own — pull me under.

Letting go will bring peace to the dissonance I feel in this world. I stop striving, for in the waters I finally meet the truth about myself that I have spent my whole life avoiding.

With my strength finally spent, I dive deep and stop trying to climb back out on my own. I surrender myself to these waters the way Jonah surrendered to the sea — trusting that even here, in the belly of my own undoing, I am not beyond being brought back to land.

As the waters close over me and I let go of my grip on my own despair, I feel the suffocation of my pride and my selfish ambitions finally give way. Peace begins to fill my heart as I stop defending myself and let the truth do what it came to do. Whether it is my own conscience, or God's conviction, or both, I know this feeling is true and right — I have needed it for a long time.

There is so much calmness in the deep, and the voices in my head begin to dissipate — but I hear another voice.

***And then I heard a voice that was not my own.***

How could there be such a calm voice in this deep sea of chaos? How could one speak to me now, when I have finally stopped holding on to anything of my own? I do not loathe myself, nor do I love myself. I wish only to be still, for I have nothing left to prove.

The voice calls out to me, but it is a voice more real and true than my own. How could it be that I hear a greater voice in the deep? Why would he call out to me? He knows the depths, and he understands everything about me.

With his voice, I feel like a child again, and I begin to smile. He knows so much more than me, and he is not like the people on the surface. He has already embraced the depths, and there is such calmness and peace in his voice. Salvation belongs to this one, and he has power over the deep.

He promises to lift me up, but I must go back to the surface and tell people about the good news I found in the depths of the water. I must help them not to fear the creatures of the deep, and help them understand that life is not found in self-preservation, or in the depths of wisdom and knowledge, but in being found by the one who holds all wisdom and knowledge. He has all authority to judge and to condemn, but he shows mercy to those willing to be baptized into his death and resurrection.

As I resurface, I find joy — not in dying, but in living as though I have already died to myself. I have been baptized into the death of Jesus the Messiah. I now know the one who died for me and caused me to be born again by his Spirit. I now know the Father, and the Father knows me, through the One who brings peace to the sea. Now I will choose to walk upon the sea, for my life is meant to be lived by faith. I will no longer chase the depths of the sea on my own, and I will no longer rely on the ships and floating cities of men. I will simply trust the One who has shown me mercy, and I will walk upon the waters with my eyes fixed on him.

---

*Scripture beneath this lament: the prayer from the deep, and salvation belonging to the LORD (Jonah 2:2–9); "out of the depths I cry to you, O LORD" (Psalm 130:1); the towers people build to keep themselves safe and undivided apart from God (Genesis 11:1–9); "whoever would save his life will lose it, and whoever loses his life for my sake will find it" (Matthew 16:25); Peter stepping out onto the water and beginning to sink when he takes his eyes off Jesus (Matthew 14:22–33); being buried with Christ in baptism and raised to walk in newness of life (Romans 6:3–4); "I have been crucified with Christ... it is no longer I who live, but Christ who lives in me" (Galatians 2:20); "fixing our eyes on Jesus" (Hebrews 12:2).*

This is the same warning [Covenantal Worship](/the-word/living-the-covenant/covenantal-worship) names directly: worship, and the examined life underneath it, was never meant to be "a descent into your own psychology, some private excavation of your inner life." That page makes the argument. This poem lives inside the temptation it describes — what it actually feels like to mistake depth of self for depth of God, and to keep diving anyway because turning back feels like defeat. The [therapeutic determinism](/the-word/knowing-your-own-way/the-cultural-currents) this corpus names elsewhere is not only a bad idea out in the culture. Taken far enough, alone, it becomes a real and dangerous undertow.

*If this poem names your own water, carry it to **[Sin, Sacrifice, and Righteousness](/the-word/living-the-covenant/sin-sacrifice-and-righteousness)**, which traces what the voice in the deep actually did about it — and to **[Come to the Table](/the-word/living-the-covenant/come-to-the-table)**, where no one is meant to carry the depths alone.*
