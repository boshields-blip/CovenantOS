# Guest Family Guide — Come to the Table

## Why this guide exists

Come to the Table is designed to be shared. A household that keeps this practice is, over time, building something worth passing on — and the most natural way to pass it on is to invite another family to the table.

This guide helps the host family prepare for that invitation well: ahead of time, graciously, and with enough simplicity that a guest family can follow along without preparation.

---

## Sending an invitation

When you invite another family to a meal where you will do Come to the Table, let them know in advance. Not an elaborate explanation — just enough that they are not surprised when a Bible is opened after the plates are filled.

A simple note, sent ahead, might say:

> *We'd love to have you for dinner this [day]. We usually read a short passage from the Bible at the table — maybe three to five minutes — and talk about it while we eat. You are welcome to listen, join in, or just enjoy the meal with us.*

That is enough. Most families, having received this kind of honest, gentle warning, arrive curious rather than anxious.

---

## A simpler first-time format

The first time another family joins the table, skip movement 5 (*Know*). Keep movements 1–4, 6, and 7. The Know slot involves the rotating categories — author, language, genre, covenantal frame, philosophy, and tradition — and it is the least necessary piece for a first guest experience.

**Simplified format for a guest table:**

| # | Movement | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Gather** | Name the passage and author; welcome the guests by name. |
| 2 | **Bless the meal** | Use the household blessing; guests are welcome to participate or simply receive. |
| 3 | **Read** | Read the passage slowly, twice if young children are present. |
| 4 | **Notice** | "What did you hear?" Round the table. Invite the guest family's children first if they are younger. Guests are welcome to pass. |
| 5 | *(skip)* | Skip the Know slot on a first visit. |
| 6 | **Live** | "In light of who God is here, how do we live this week?" Name one invitation. Guests are welcome to take it home if it resonates. |
| 7 | **Bless and send** | A short closing blessing. |

The session takes less time with the Know slot removed. That is fine. The first visit is about welcome, not content.

---

## The host script for a guest table

The host's opening words set the tone. Something like:

*"We read a short passage at the table every week. You are welcome to listen, to read, or just to eat. There are no wrong answers and no test. We'll go around the table and ask what everyone heard."*

Then proceed. Do not explain the practice further. The practice explains itself.

---

## Inviting the guest family's children

One of the most meaningful moments at a guest table often comes from the guest family's children. They have heard none of the prior sessions, they have no accumulated framework, and they often say the most direct thing anyone at the table needed to hear.

Invite them in just as you would your own children: *"[Name], what's a word you heard?"* If they pass, that is fine. If they engage, receive it fully.

---

## The hard rule: no Berean critique at a guest table

When another family is present, the Berean Tool stays away. No critique of a teacher, sermon, tradition, or theological position that may belong to the guest family.

This is not a rule born of conflict avoidance. It is a rule born of hospitality. A guest has come to the table. They have not signed up for a diagnostic conversation about their tradition or their trusted teachers.

The table is a place of formation, not contention. This rule holds even when the Berean Tool would be genuinely useful and even when the guest family seems comfortable. Keep it for another occasion.

---

## Sending the practice home

Near the end of the evening — or as guests are leaving — offer to send them home with something simple. A handwritten note of the passage, the session's live invitation, and a brief description of the practice is enough.

A simple card might say:

> *Tonight's passage: [reference]. Tonight's invitation: [live invitation]. If you'd like to try this at your own table, the format is: Gather → Bless the meal → Read → Notice → Know → Live → Bless and send.*

No pressure, no follow-up required. You are simply offering the practice to another household.

Some families will receive it and set it aside. Some will try it. A few will keep it. The point is to make the practice available — not to recruit.

---

## When the guest family wants to know more

If a guest family is curious about the practice and wants to learn more, the simplest next step is to invite them again. Multiple shared meals together will explain the practice better than any document can.

If they want a resource to take home, point them to [SESSION_FORMAT.md](./SESSION_FORMAT.md) and [HOST_GUIDE.md](./HOST_GUIDE.md). These two files contain enough to begin.

For a family with no background in Scripture, a gentle starting point is the passage you read together that evening — a single text they already know something about because they were present when it was read.
