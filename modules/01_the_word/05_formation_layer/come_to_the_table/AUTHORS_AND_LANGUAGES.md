# Authors and Languages — Come to the Table

**Status:** Draft | **Author:** Brandon O'Shields

---

## The rotating "Know" slot

Movement 5 of every session (*Know*) is a single, brief teaching moment offered by the host. It adds *one thing* about the passage — not a summary, not a commentary, but a small piece of context that helps the table hear the text more truly.

The Know slot rotates through seven categories. No category is used two sessions in a row. Over the course of a year, the household will encounter each category multiple times, always in connection with a passage they have actually heard.

---

## The seven categories

### 1. Author

*Who wrote this?*

A sentence or two about the human author: who they were, where they lived, what shaped them. No extensive biography — just enough to make the author a person rather than a name.

**Examples:**
- *"Moses wrote this. He grew up as an Egyptian prince and spent forty years in the wilderness before God called him back."*
- *"Paul wrote this letter while he was in prison."*
- *"We don't know exactly who wrote Hebrews. Many have guessed, but the author left no name."*

Over years of the Author category, the household will meet Moses, David, Isaiah, Luke, Paul, John, Peter, James, and others — not as abstractions, but as persons with histories.

### 2. Audience

*Who first heard or received this?*

A sentence about the original recipients: who they were, what their situation was, what the author may have wanted them to understand or do.

**Examples:**
- *"Paul is writing to a small mixed community in Rome — Jews and Gentiles who did not always get along."*
- *"The Psalms were sung by God's people in worship. People who were afraid, grieving, grateful, or confused sang these exact words."*
- *"Revelation was written to seven churches in what is now western Turkey. They were under pressure and some were beginning to compromise."*

### 3. Language note

*One Hebrew or Greek word, said aloud.*

Pick one significant word from the passage and share a brief note about what the original language carries that the translation may partially hide. Say the word aloud. Let the household hear it.

**Examples:**
- *"The Hebrew word for 'spirit' in this passage is 'ruach.' It also means breath and wind. Say it: ruach."*
- *"The Greek word for 'love' here is 'agape.' It means a chosen, active care — not a feeling that comes and goes."*
- *"The Hebrew word 'shalom' means something closer to wholeness and right order than to the absence of conflict."*

This category is not meant to make the household fluent in biblical languages. It is meant to make them curious about the original words — and to give them a small vocabulary they can return to.

### 4. Genre

*What kind of writing is this?*

A sentence about the literary form: is this poetry, narrative, prophecy, letter, wisdom, apocalyptic, law? The genre shapes how the passage should be heard.

**Examples:**
- *"This is a song. God's people sang it. It is not trying to give us a formula — it is trying to give us a way to pray."*
- *"This is a letter written to a specific community. Paul is not writing universal instructions; he is responding to a particular problem."*
- *"This is apocalyptic writing — it uses images and visions to say something about what is real, not to give a timeline."*

### 5. Covenantal frame

*Where does this passage sit in the story of God with His people?*

A sentence about context in the redemptive arc: what has just happened before this passage, what has not happened yet, and how that shapes what the passage is saying.

**Examples:**
- *"This comes after the exile. The people have lost everything — land, temple, king. Isaiah is writing into that specific grief."*
- *"This is early in Genesis. God has not yet called Abraham. There is no Israel, no law, no temple. This is the story before any of that."*
- *"This letter was written within thirty years of the resurrection. The early church is still figuring out what happened."*

Over time, the household builds a living sense of the canonical arc — not as a timeline memorized, but as a story inhabited.

### 6. Philosophy point

*What does this passage say about one of the seven fundamental questions?*

A gentle pointer to the Diagnostic Layer's seven categories: metaphysics, epistemology, anthropology, ethics, social/political, identity/meaning, eschatology. Name the category without necessarily requiring everyone at the table to memorize it at once.

See [../../02_diagnostic_engine/README.md](../../02_diagnostic_engine/README.md) for the full treatment of each category.

**Examples:**
- *"This passage says something about what a human being is. According to this, what are we made for?"* (Anthropology)
- *"This passage is saying something about how we know what is true — where we get our knowledge from."* (Epistemology)
- *"This tells us where history is going. There is an end, and God is steering toward it."* (Eschatology)

A child who hears this category over years will eventually learn the names of the categories. More importantly, they will have concrete passages attached to each one — not definitions, but examples.

### 7. Living tradition

*How have God's people read this for a long time?*

A sentence about how the church has received, prayed, sung, argued about, or lived from this passage across generations. This does not require detailed historical knowledge — a single anecdote, phrase, or tradition is enough.

**Examples:**
- *"Christians have prayed this Psalm at night for almost two thousand years. It has been part of evening prayer in many traditions."*
- *"This passage was central to the early church's arguments about who Jesus is. It was not a minor text — people staked their lives on understanding it correctly."*
- *"This chapter was memorized by many families in the early American church. Some households still have it posted in their homes."*

## Why one thing per session

The discipline of the Know slot is the discipline of *one*. Not three things. Not a short homily. One.

Over a four-year arc at roughly fifty sessions a year, that is approximately two hundred single pieces of information offered at the table. Each is attached to a passage actually heard, in the presence of bread, plates, siblings, and ordinary life.

A child who shares this table from age five through age eighteen will have heard something about the author, language, genre, covenantal frame, philosophy, and tradition of roughly two hundred passages — without ever having been sat down for a lecture.

That is the point of the Know slot. Not information transfer. Formation by accumulation, in the context of the meal.
