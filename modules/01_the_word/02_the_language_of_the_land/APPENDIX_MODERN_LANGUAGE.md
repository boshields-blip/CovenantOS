# Appendix — Packaged Words, Grown Words

**Status:** Draft (reference) | **Author:** Brandon O'Shields | **Last updated:** 2026-08-12

---

> This is a reference appendix, not part of the main path. Learn the language of the land first ([Hebrew Word Pictures](/the-word/the-language-of-the-land/hebrew-word-pictures), [Thinking in Blocks](/the-word/the-language-of-the-land/thinking-in-blocks)); this page is here for when you want to notice the pull of your own tongue by contrast.

Once you have begun to hear how the Bible's own language thinks — concrete, embodied, covenantal — you start to notice how far modern speech has drifted from it. Here is the simplest way I have found to say why: words are like food. Some words are packaged — made somewhere else, by someone else, sealed up and shipped to you ready to use, no growing required. Other words are grown — worked out of an actual encounter with the text, your land, your household, your neighbor. [Household vs. Consumer Economy](/the-household/household-vs-consumer-economy) makes this same case about an egg. This page makes it about your vocabulary.

Language tutors perception the same way food shapes a body. A church that only ever reaches for packaged words will slowly come to imagine itself the way those words imagine it. This is not about policing anyone's speech — most people reach for packaged words without any ill intent, because packaged words are simply what is sitting on the shelf (see [The Cultural Currents](/the-word/knowing-your-own-way/the-cultural-currents)). The aim here is just to notice what you are eating.

## Packaged words

Packaged words arrive pre-made. They were assembled somewhere you have never been — a boardroom, a therapist's office, a marketing meeting, a platform's growth team — and shipped to you ready to use. You can say them without ever having encountered the thing they claim to describe. That is exactly their appeal: they are fast, they sound current, and everyone around you is already eating them.

You can hear packaged words at work whenever "the church" starts sounding like a company with a brand to protect and a pipeline to optimize, or like a movement finding her voice and rising — both are the same packaging in different wrapping, and both quietly replace an actual congregation of actual people with an abstraction that needs managing or cheering on. Packaged words are not sinful. They are simply not yours. You did not grow them, so they cannot really feed you — only fill you.

## Grown words

Grown words cost you something before you ever say them. They come from time spent in the text until a word like *hesed* or *shalom* stops being a vocabulary item and starts being something you have actually tasted. They come from time spent on your own land, in your own trade, at your own table, with the actual neighbor in front of you rather than the neighbor a screen shows you. They are often slower to reach for and less polished than the packaged version. They also happen to be true, in the way that food you grew yourself is simply, undeniably food, whatever else can be said about it.

This is the whole aim of learning to think in the language of the land: not to collect a better vocabulary, but to grow your own.

## A few pairs

Packaged: "We need to scale the ministry." Grown: "How do we strengthen this work without outrunning the people in it?" One was made in a boardroom. The other has dirt on it.

Packaged: "I need to protect my peace." Grown: "I need to pursue peace with truth, and with the person I am avoiding." One manages a feeling. The other does the work peace actually requires.

Packaged: "My identity is my story." Grown: "My identity is what God has named me, inside a story I did not write alone." One makes you the author. The other makes you a son or a daughter.

Packaged: "The church should meet my needs." Grown: "I belong to a people who bear one another's burdens, mine included." One treats the congregation like a vendor. The other treats it like a household.

Packaged: "We need a platform." Grown: "We need a trustworthy presence and a life worth watching." One is built. The other is grown, slowly, in view of people who already know you.

## Using this well

The cure is not cynicism about language, and it is certainly not a new legalism that polices every word anyone reaches for under pressure. It is the patient work of growing your own — which happens not by scolding yourself out of packaged words but by spending real time in the language of the land. The more you learn to think in *dabar* and *hesed* and *shalom*, the more the packaged words will start to taste like what they are: something made far away, by someone who never met you, for a shelf, not a table.
