# Thinking in Blocks

**Status:** Draft | **Author:** Brandon O'Shields | **Last updated:** 2026-08-01

---

It is not only the *words* of Scripture that differ from ours. It is the way the mind behind them *reasons*. The Western tradition, shaped by Greek philosophy, tends to think in straight lines and systems: define your terms, resolve every tension, arrange the truths into a logical order with no loose ends. The Hebrew mind thinks differently — in images, in stories, in truths laid side by side and held together without being flattened into a system. Learning to read Scripture well means learning to reason a little more the way it reasons.

## Block logic: truth held in tension

Hebrew thought often places two truths side by side — like two blocks set next to each other — and lets them stand in tension rather than resolving them into a tidy formula. Both are true. The tension is not a problem to be solved; it is the way the fuller truth is held.

Scripture is full of this. God is sovereign over all things, *and* human beings are truly responsible. God is one, *and* He makes Himself known as Father, Son, and Spirit. Pharaoh hardens his own heart, *and* God hardens Pharaoh's heart. The Western instinct is to pick one block and explain away the other, or to build an elaborate system reconciling them. The Hebrew instinct is to hold both, and to worship. Much of the frustration modern readers feel with the Bible comes from demanding a kind of systematic resolution the text was never trying to give. It is content to lay the blocks side by side and let them stand.

To read this way is not to abandon your mind. It is to accept that reality — and the God who made it — is larger than any system we can close, and that some truths are held most faithfully in tension.

## Parallelism: saying it twice to say it fuller

Hebrew poetry — and nearly a third of the Bible is poetry — does not rhyme sounds. It rhymes *ideas*. A line says something, and the next line says it again from a slightly different angle. This is called parallelism, and once you see it, the Psalms and Proverbs open up.

Sometimes the second line simply echoes the first: "The heavens declare the glory of God; the skies proclaim the work of his hands." Sometimes it sharpens by contrast: "The LORD watches over the way of the righteous, but the way of the wicked leads to destruction." Sometimes it builds and intensifies. The point is that meaning lives in the *relationship between the lines*, not in one line alone. When you read Hebrew poetry, slow down and ask how the second line is deepening, sharpening, or extending the first. The truth is in the pairing.

## Story before system

The Bible teaches most of its deepest truths not through definitions but through narrative. Ask the Bible "what is faith?" and it does not hand you a definition; it tells you about Abraham leaving his country. Ask "what is covenant loyalty?" and it gives you Ruth in a barley field. Ask "what is God like?" and it tells you the exodus, the exile, the cross.

This is not because the biblical writers were incapable of abstraction. It is because they knew that some truths can only be *told*, not defined — that a story forms you in a way a definition never can. The modern reader is tempted to strip the story down to its "principle" and discard the narrative as mere packaging. The Hebrew mind knows the story *is* the teaching. To read well is to dwell in the narrative and let it work, rather than rushing to extract a takeaway.

## Both-and, not either-or

Put these habits together and a posture emerges. The Hebrew mind is more comfortable with both-and than the modern mind, which craves either-or. Is a person body or soul? Both — a living body given breath. Is salvation God's work or does it call for my response? Both. Is the kingdom of God here now or still coming? Both. Is the Bible for the head or the heart or the hands? All three, always together.

This is not fuzzy thinking or a refusal to be clear. It is a refusal to amputate half the truth for the sake of a cleaner system. And it guards against one of the deepest modern habits — technocratic rationalism's assumption that everything real can be fully systematized and controlled (see [The Cultural Currents](/the-word/knowing-your-own-way/the-cultural-currents)). Some of the most important things are held, not solved.

## Reading with the grain

To read Scripture with the grain of its own mind, then, is to slow down and change a few reflexes: to hold tensions instead of rushing to resolve them, to read poetry as paired and layered rather than flat, to dwell in stories instead of stripping them for principles, and to accept both-and where you wanted either-or.

None of this is difficult, but all of it is countercultural, because it runs against the systematizing, abstracting habits most of us were trained in. That is exactly why it is worth practicing. When you begin to think in blocks, in parallels, and in stories, you are no longer standing outside the text translating it into your categories. You are beginning to think inside it.
