# Corresponding Strengths — A Dyad Study

---

> Then the LORD God said, "It is not good that the man should be alone; I will make him a helper fit for him [*ezer kenegdo*]." — Genesis 2:18

This study grew out of a simple question: why should a household need something like CliftonStrengths at all? Those tools are not evil, but they were built to answer a different question than the one a marriage actually asks. They measure the individual — your talents, ranked, so you can manage your career or your team. A marriage is not a team of two individuals managing each other. It is one flesh made of two strengths, facing each other. So this study measures the pair, not the person. The question is never *what are you good at.* It is *what has God knit the two of you to carry together, and where does that carrying actually happen* — in the work, the garden, the table, the door held open, the trade, the neighbor.

One more thing, and it matters more than it sounds like it should: this is not a typing tool. It will not tell you that you are an Encourager and your wife is a Strategic. Naming a spouse with a fixed label is its own quiet danger — you can end up relating to the label instead of the person, which is the very failure [The Two and the One](/the-word/walking-the-text/the-two-and-the-one) already names as the oldest wound between man and woman: seeing an image instead of a face. So hold whatever you learn here loosely. Come back to it a season from now and expect the answers to have moved, because you will have moved.

## How to walk through this

Do this together, at the table, not apart. Each of you reads a domain, answers alone in a few honest sentences — written, not just thought — then you trade and read each other's answers out loud. Talk about what surprised you. One domain a sitting is plenty; five in an evening will make you rush past the parts worth staying in. Nothing here is scored. There is no report at the end, no percentile, no total. The only output is two people who see each other a little more clearly than they did an hour before.

## What you were each given

Before you were a household, you were two people, formed before either of you had a say in it — a temperament, a body, a set of instincts, a family that shaped you before you could evaluate it. *Ezer kenegdo* does not mean two identical strengths; it means a strength that corresponds — that meets the other's, sometimes by matching it, more often by covering the ground it doesn't reach.

**Ask each other:** What did I bring into this marriage that I never chose and can't take credit for — a steadiness, a restlessness, a way of noticing things, a way of pushing through pain? Where has your strength quietly been covering a place I am weak, longer than either of us has said out loud?

**A caution:** Don't answer with a trait word (steady, driven, nurturing) and stop there. Say where it actually shows up — a moment, a decision, a Tuesday.

**What arises from the land of the text:** You were each formed before the other met you, by a God who does not make interchangeable parts. The correspondence was there before the wedding. Marriage is where you finally see it.

---

## Work & craft

The household is not held up by feelings; it is held up by labor. Someone earns, someone builds, someone repairs, someone learns a trade well enough to be trusted with it. Both may work outside the home, both may work within it — the question is not who does which job, but where your two labors actually interlock.

**Ask each other:** Where does my work depend on yours, in ways I don't say thank you for often enough? When something breaks — the car, the budget, the schedule, a relationship — who moves first, and what does the other one do while they wait?

**A caution:** Resist sorting this into "provider" and "supporter." Both of you are laboring. Name the actual shape of it in your house, not the shape you assume you're supposed to have.

**What arises from the land of the text:** *Avodah* — work and worship are the same word in Hebrew. Your labor, joined to your spouse's, is not merely functional. It is liturgy for two.

---

## Provision & table

Someone plans meals, someone cooks, someone remembers what everyone needs and when. This domain is about who tends what gets put into bodies and what gets said around the table — because the table is where the household actually becomes visible to itself.

**Ask each other:** What do I do, without being asked, to make sure this household is fed — not just with food, but with attention, correction, blessing? What happens at our table that would stop happening if I disappeared for a month?

**A caution:** If one of you does nearly all of this, say so plainly. This is a place to notice weight, not just to celebrate strength.

**What arises from the land of the text:** Provision was never meant to be private consumption. It becomes formation at the table — gratitude, story, correction, memory — see [Household Table](/the-household/household-flow-model).

---

## Hospitality

The table opens outward, or it doesn't. Some households welcome easily and struggle to sustain it; others are slower to open the door but go deep once it's open. A marriage usually carries both instincts, unevenly distributed.

**Ask each other:** Who usually notices the person standing outside the circle — at church, on the street, at a gathering — and moves toward them first? When we host, what am I actually doing while you're doing the thing you do well?

**A caution:** Don't let the more outgoing spouse's gift eclipse the quieter one's. Slow hospitality — the kind that shows up with a meal three weeks after the crisis, when everyone else has stopped calling — is a real strength, not the absence of one.

**What arises from the land of the text:** Hospitality converts private abundance into public mercy. Two different instincts for it, joined, cover more ground than either could alone.

---

## Trade & exchange

Money, negotiation, fairness with people outside the household — this is where a lot of marriages quietly fracture, because it's the domain most exposed to the world's terms instead of the household's. Naming your actual strengths here, honestly, heads off a fight before it starts.

**Ask each other:** When we deal with money, or a contractor, or a hard negotiation, who sees the numbers clearly and who sees the person clearly — and have we ever let both of those seeings sit in the same room before deciding?

**A caution:** This is the domain most likely to turn into blame ("you're bad with money," "you're too soft"). Reframe every answer as *where does the other's strength need to be let in*, not where they fall short.

**What arises from the land of the text:** Trade is good when it serves people instead of dissolving them (see [Household Flow Model](/the-household/household-flow-model)). A household negotiates that faithfully with two sets of eyes, not one.

---

## Commons & community

Every household eventually has to answer for more than itself — aging parents, a struggling neighbor, a church that needs hands, a friend in crisis. This is where a marriage's strengths get exported into the wider covenant life the whole of Covenant Architecture is finally pointed toward.

**Ask each other:** Where have we, together, actually shown up for someone outside our four walls this year? Whose instinct pulled us toward that person — and what did the other one add once we got there?

**A caution:** Don't inventory this as a list of good deeds. Notice the pattern — who sees the need first, who sustains the follow-through — because that pattern is the actual strength.

**What arises from the land of the text:** A household that cannot see across its own table will not see across the road. What you practice here scales outward into [The Compact](/the-compact).

---

## Returning, not filing

Don't file this away. The whole point is that a marriage is not a fixed thing to diagnose once — it's two people still being formed, individually and together, and the shape of your corresponding strengths at year three will not be the shape at year thirteen. Walk through it again next season. Notice what moved. That movement, tracked honestly over years, is a better picture of your marriage than any report a strengths test could hand you in twenty minutes.

*A note for later:* this same pattern — receive, ask, caution against the label, ground in the text — could widen out to other dyads a household actually lives inside: parent and child, pastor and household, neighbor and neighbor. That is a study for another season. Start here, with the oldest dyad, and let it be enough for now.
