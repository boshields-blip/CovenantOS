# The Household Hollowed

**Status:** Draft | **Author:** Brandon O'Shields | **Last updated:** 2026-08-01

---

Before we can talk about what a household is *for*, we have to name what happened to it — because most of us are trying to build a home on the ruins of one, without quite knowing that a demolition took place. The unease so many men and women feel in their own houses is not a personal failure and it is not imagined. It is the aftershock of a structural change so large that it reshaped what it even means to be a man, a woman, and a family. And almost no one told us it happened.

## The home used to be a workshop

For most of human history, the household was a *productive* place — the center of an actual economy, as [Oikonomia](/the-household/oikonomia) and the [household-versus-consumer contrast](/the-household/household-vs-consumer-economy) describe. Food was grown and preserved there. Cloth was made there. Children were taught there. Tools were built and mended there. Trade went out from there. A home was a workshop, a school, a farm, and a small business woven into one, and every member of it — man, woman, child, grandparent, hired hand — had real, visible, valued work inside a shared enterprise.

Then, over a couple of generations, the productive work left. The factory drew labor out of the home and into a distant workplace. What had been one interwoven life split into two disconnected halves: a *workplace* out there where value was made and measured, and a *home* back here that no longer produced anything the wider world counted. The house stopped being a workshop and slowly became a place you return to after the "real" work is done — a place of rest, consumption, and private life. This is the great inversion, and once it happened, everything built on the home's logic quietly changed with it.

The point here is not nostalgia. You cannot un-invent the modern economy, and no one is asking you to. The point is *sight* — to see clearly what was lost, so you can recover what can be recovered rather than blaming yourself for an ache the culture handed you.

## What it did to men

When productive work left the home, so did the man — first for most of his waking hours, and then, slowly, in imagination. His worth relocated to the workplace, where it was measured by performance, competition, and output. Home became the place he was *from*, not the place he *labored*; the arena where he mattered was elsewhere.

This is why so many good men feel a low, persistent sense of being unnecessary at home — present in the evenings but peripheral to the household's real functioning, unsure what their embodied role even is once "provider" is reduced to a paycheck deposited from afar. Some respond by pouring themselves into work or competition, where the scoreboard is clear. Some disengage. Some, feeling the vacuum of authority, try to fill it with control — grasping for a dominance that the older, presence-based fatherhood never needed, because it was rooted in shared work and daily, visible responsibility rather than in command. None of these is the embodied, present, sacrificial masculinity Scripture actually describes. They are the shapes a man contorts into when the ground his fathers stood on has been pulled out from under him.

## What it did to women

The same event fell on women from the other side. Historically the woman was central to the household economy — a producer of real and recognized value: food, textiles, education, care, the management of a complex working enterprise. When the home stopped producing, her sphere was redefined as a *non*-economic, private, "domestic" space — and a culture that measures worth by market output quietly recoded her essential work as unproductive, sentimental, and socially invisible.

This is why so many women feel undervalued and isolated in a way they can hardly name: asked to hold together the emotional and relational life of a family, often alone, inside a house society no longer treats as a place where anything of value is made. The work is endless and the recognition is thin. Some carry an exhausting emotional labor without any structural weight or honor to match it; some feel pressed to prove their worth by leaving the home for the workplace where value is at least counted; some feel guilty either way. None of this reflects the strong, honored, producing woman of Scripture — the *ezer* strength, the Proverbs 31 household that hums with enterprise and dignity. It reflects a home that was hollowed out beneath her.

## The shared ache — and why it matters

Here is the thing worth sitting with: **men and women are not, at root, each other's problem here. They are fellow casualties of the same demolition.** The husband who feels unnecessary and the wife who feels unseen are both grieving the loss of the same thing — a shared, meaningful, productive common life in which both of them had honored, visible work and real interdependence. The modern "battle of the sexes" is, in large part, two wounded people blaming each other for a wound the economy inflicted on them both.

That reframing is itself a small healing, because it lets a couple stop fighting each other and start seeing each other — which, as [To See](/the-word/walking-the-text/to-see) and [The Two and the One](/the-word/walking-the-text/the-two-and-the-one) argue, is where everything begins. And it names the real culprit honestly: not men, not women, but the [cultural currents](/the-word/knowing-your-own-way/the-cultural-currents) — consumerism and technocratic industrialism — that hollowed the home and then handed each sex a caricature of the other to fight.

> *A word from the author.* I felt this hollowing in my own home long before I could name it. It was not until I began to work my own land and eat food from my own garden — beside my wife and my children — that my eyes were opened to how far we had drifted, and to how much could still be recovered. This is not theory to me. It is the road I am still walking.

## What was actually lost

Strip it down and the loss is threefold:

- **Shared work.** The home stopped being a place where a family makes anything together, and so stopped being a place where every member has honored, visible, interdependent labor.
- **Recognized worth.** Value was relocated to the market, and everything the home does that the market cannot price — formation, care, hospitality, presence — was quietly demoted to "not real work."
- **Interdependence.** When production left, so did the daily, mutual need that had bound a household together. Members became co-residents sharing a consumption space rather than co-laborers sharing an enterprise.

You cannot rebuild all of it, and you do not need to. But you can recover more of it than you think — and even a little recovery changes everything, because the ache was never really about chores or roles. It was about *meaning*, *worth*, and *being needed by one another*. That is what the next page takes up: not a rulebook for the home, but a vision for filling it back up.
